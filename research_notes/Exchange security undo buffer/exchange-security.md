# Exchange and off-ramp security for a Solana USDC family transfer

Scope for the report writer: Miami hackathon family-wallet demo, not a worldwide neobank. Research cutoff is 7 November 2026. Facts below are what the cited page says. Items under Inferences are not in the sources. USDC is not described here as an insured bank deposit. A Solana transfer that has reached finalized commitment is not described as reversible.

## What are the main loss modes at a crypto exchange or fiat on/off-ramp, with dated 2024–2026 primary sources?

### Takeaway
The documented failures are unauthorized withdrawal after someone else can sign or log in, a payment to an address or account the sender did not mean, and issuer or bank action that locks or returns funds after they have left the user. A completed on-chain USDC send is not an undo. Fiat rails can still fail or come back after the crypto deposit has already been taken.

### Cited Findings
- Account takeover, 25 November 2025: FBI IC3 PSA I-112525-style alert `PSA251125` says that since January 2025 IC3 received more than 5,100 account-takeover complaints, with losses exceeding $262 million. Criminals impersonate a financial institution by text, call, email, or a fake site, collect login credentials plus an MFA or one-time code, reset the password, and lock the owner out. The PSA says they then wire funds to criminal-controlled accounts, many linked to cryptocurrency wallets, and that those funds are disbursed quickly and are difficult to trace and recover. The PSA is about financial-institution, payroll, and HSA accounts, not a named crypto exchange. — [FBI IC3, Account Takeover Fraud via Impersonation of Financial Institution Support](https://www.ic3.gov/psa/2025/PSA251125)
- Cryptocurrency complaint totals for 2025: the FBI IC3 2025 annual report records 181,565 cryptocurrency complaints and $11.366 billion in losses, a 22% increase in losses from 2024, with an average loss of $62,604. It says cryptocurrency investment fraud was the highest source of financial losses to Americans in 2025, at $7.2 billion (a chart line reads $7.228 billion). The same report describes investment-club and pig-butchering style scams in which victims send cryptocurrency to fake platforms and then cannot withdraw. — [FBI IC3 2025 annual report (PDF)](https://www.ic3.gov/annualreport/reports/2025_ic3report.pdf)
- Phishing of signers, 21 February 2025: Bybit’s own timeline says that at 14:13 UTC on 21 February 2025, during a routine move from an Ethereum multisig cold wallet to a warm wallet, attackers exploited the Safe{Wallet} UI so signers approved a transaction that changed the cold wallet’s smart-contract logic. Bybit says one cold wallet was lost, totaling $1.46 billion: 401,347 ETH, 90,375 stETH, 15,000 cmETH, and 8,000 mETH, split across 39 addresses. Bybit attributes the exploit to the Lazarus Group. Bybit also says that by 23 February 2025, $42.89 million of exploited funds had been frozen with help from partners including Circle, and that mETH Protocol recovered 15,000 cmETH. — [Bybit, Security Incident timeline](https://www.bybit.com/en/learn/this-week-in-bybit/bybit-security-incident-timeline)
- Same incident, company forensics, 26 February 2025: Bybit’s press release says a forensic review concluded that a Safe developer’s credentials were compromised, which let the attacker deceive Bybit signers into approving a malicious transaction. Bybit says Verichains and Sygnia found no compromise of Bybit’s own infrastructure, and that Bybit moved most funds out of Safe-administered addresses the day of the incident. — [Bybit press release, 26 February 2025](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/bybit-confirms-security-integrity-amid-safe-wallet-incident--no-compromise-in-infrastructure-302386274.html)
- Same incident, wallet vendor, 28 February 2025: the Safe Ecosystem Foundation published the Safe{Wallet} team’s statement that the attack was achieved through a compromised Safe{Wallet} developer machine, which proposed a disguised malicious transaction. The statement says external forensic review did not indicate vulnerabilities in the Safe smart contracts or in the frontend and services source code, that infrastructure was rebuilt and credentials rotated, and that users still need to be extremely cautious when signing. — [Safe Ecosystem Foundation statement, 28 February 2025](https://safefoundation.org/blog/safe-ecosystem-foundation-statement)
- Same incident, regulator, 26 February 2025: FBI PSA `I-022625-PSA` says North Korea was responsible for the theft of approximately $1.5 billion in virtual assets from Bybit on or about 21 February 2025, under the name TraderTraitor. The FBI says some stolen assets had already been converted to bitcoin and other assets across thousands of addresses, and it asked exchanges, RPC operators, bridges, and other virtual-asset service providers to block transactions with addresses used to launder them. — [FBI IC3 PSA, 26 February 2025](https://www.ic3.gov/psa/2025/psa250226)
- Hot-wallet / provider compromise, as described by the issuer on 5 November 2026: Circle’s blog says a reported attack on Binance appears to involve more than $6 billion in digital assets, with the FBI attributing it to actors connected to North Korea. Circle says the investigation is ongoing and the facts will continue to develop. Circle says it froze approximately $1.1 billion in USDC associated with the incident in coordination with law enforcement. Circle says a freeze, done when law enforcement has a valid court order, prevents USDC from moving in or out of that address, and that Circle does not decide who owns the assets, whether they should be forfeited, or whether a crime occurred. — [Circle, 5 November 2026](https://www.circle.com/blog/the-binance-incident-collective-defense-must-move-at-machine-speed)
- Wrong address and no recall: Circle’s USDC Terms, last updated 15 October 2026, say USDC transactions are not reversible, and that once USDC is sent the sender accepts the risk of losing access indefinitely or permanently. The listed examples include an address entered incorrectly whose owner is never found, loss of the private key, and a recipient who will not return the USDC or who will return it only after identity verification. A separate heading says an on-chain send to a third-party address is irreversible and that Circle does not have the ability to reverse or recall any transaction once initiated. — [Circle USDC Terms, updated 15 October 2026](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-terms)
- Frozen stablecoin and forfeited redemption: the same 15 October 2026 terms say Circle may block addresses and, if the address is Circle-custodied, freeze associated USDC temporarily or permanently when Circle decides it may be associated with illegal activity or a terms violation. Sending to or receiving from a blocked address can lead to a freeze, account termination, and forfeiture of redemption rights. Circle may also be required to freeze USDC and surrender dollars held in segregated accounts if a valid government authority orders it. Blocklisting is described as a right to block on-chain transfer of USDC to and from an address as permitted under the blocklisting policy. — [Circle USDC Terms, updated 15 October 2026](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-terms)
- Not deposit insurance: the 15 October 2026 terms say USDC held in a Circle Mint account is not subject to FDIC or SIPC protection in the United States, and not subject to the UK Financial Services Compensation Scheme or an equivalent scheme elsewhere. The older public risk-factors page, last updated 27 October 2023, says the same about a “USDC Account,” and still describes some mechanics as Ethereum-only. The 2026 terms are the later Circle text and speak of “USDC Supported Blockchains,” not Ethereum alone. — [Circle USDC Terms](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-terms); [Circle USDC Risk Factors, updated 27 October 2023](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-risk-factors)
- Fiat-rail failure after crypto has moved: Bridge’s transfer-state doc says a transfer that reaches `payment_processed` has delivered funds to the destination and “can never go backwards” along `awaiting_funds` → `funds_received` → `payment_submitted` → `payment_processed`. Exception states include `undeliverable`, `returned` (payment was sent but failed; funds have returned to Bridge and a refund is underway), `refund_failed`, and `error`. Bridge says it can refund fiat deposits only to the original sending account, and `refund_failed` usually means that account is closed, deactivated, or suspended. — [Bridge, Transfer states](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/platform/orchestration/transfers/transfer-states)
- Crossmint documents a bank rejection after it has already received the stablecoin: the order is `phase: "completed"` with `delivery.status: "failed"`, and “the buyer deposit is automatically refunded,” with `payment.refunded` set. One documented code is `invalid-account`. A separate case says if a deposit confirms only after the quote expires, Crossmint refunds it to the payer wallet instead of paying out at a stale rate (`failureReason.code: "payment-refunded"`). The create-order schema says `completedAt` is kept if a completed payout later fails, and gives a bank return as the example. On instant rails including SPEI, `completedAt` is described as the credit confirmation time. — [Crossmint, Manage Orders](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/guides/manage-orders); [Crossmint, Create Order](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/api-reference/create-order)

### Inferences
- The Bybit loss was a signing failure, not a Solana finality failure. Signers approved a transaction the UI did not honestly display. A family app that asks a parent to sign a sponsored Solana transfer has the same class of risk if the app, wallet, or fee relayer can substitute the instruction before the signature.
- Circle’s November 2026 freeze of about $1.1 billion USDC did not pull those tokens back to the theft victims. Circle’s own wording is that the freeze stops USDC moving in or out of the blocked address, and that ownership and forfeiture are for law enforcement and courts. That is a different control from recalling a family member’s already finalized transfer.
- FBI account-takeover numbers are not a count of crypto-exchange hacks. They are evidence that a fiat account used to on-ramp, once taken over, can be emptied into crypto-linked accounts faster than a recall helps. The IC3 investment-fraud figures are a different loss mode: the victim authorizes the send.
- For this demo, the practical loss sequence is: local fiat leaves a bank or exchange account, USDC arrives on Solana and cannot be recalled, then an off-ramp either pays the wrong CLABE, pays and later gets a bank return, or fails and depends on the provider to refund. Each hop has a different undo, and the Solana hop has none.

### Gaps
- No Binance or FBI primary document confirming the size, the entry point, or the asset list of the November 2026 Binance incident was retrieved. Circle’s 5 November 2026 post is explicit that the investigation is ongoing and that “more than $6 billion” is what the reported attack “appears to involve.”
- No 2024–2026 primary write-up from Dunamu/Upbit, DMM Bitcoin, or a Japanese or Korean regulator was retrieved. News articles about those cases were not used as findings.
- No Coinbase, Kraken, or similar exchange help page was fetched for their own withdrawal-irreversibility or account-takeover language. Wrong-address irreversibility above is Circle’s, plus the Solana finality sources in the next section.
- The Circle Stablecoin Access Denial Policy PDF that was fetched has no visible publication date in the extracted text.

## For Solana USDC, once a transfer is confirmed, can it be recalled, and what does Circle say about freezing, blacklisting, and recall?

### Takeaway
Circle says it cannot reverse or recall a USDC transaction once initiated. What Circle can do is block an address so USDC controlled by that address cannot be transferred on-chain, and freeze USDC at a Circle-custodied address. On Solana, as of the Alpenglow activation on 21 October 2026, Anza’s commitment docs say a confirmed transaction and a finalized transaction are the same thing: permanently committed and not rolled back. USDC is not deposit insurance.

### Cited Findings
- Recall: Circle USDC Terms, last updated 15 October 2026: “When USDC is sent to a third-party USDC address, such transaction is completed on USDC Supported Blockchains. This means that such transaction is irreversible and Circle does not have the ability to reverse or recall any transaction once initiated.” The terms also say Circle is not obligated to track or determine the provenance of USDC balances. — [Circle USDC Terms](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-terms)
- The public risk-factors page, last updated 27 October 2023, uses the same “does not have the ability to reverse or recall” sentence, but it still says the transaction “is completed on the Ethereum blockchain” and that “USDC is based on open source software and runs on the Ethereum blockchain.” The 15 October 2026 terms replaced that with “USDC Supported Blockchains.” Both pages are live. The 2026 terms are the later statement. — [Circle USDC Risk Factors](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-risk-factors); [Circle USDC Terms](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-terms)
- Block versus freeze, terms: “block” applies to certain USDC addresses. Freeze of the associated USDC, temporary or permanent, is stated for addresses that are Circle-custodied. Separately, “Blocklisting” says Circle “reserves the right to block the transfer of USDC to and from an address on chain as permitted under the blocklisting policy.” Redemption of 1 USD per USDC with Circle is only for “Users Type A,” and is conditional on holding the USDC in a registered Circle Mint account, not violating the terms, and no regulator, law-enforcement, or court action restricting redemption. Sending USDC to another address assigns the redemption right to that later holder only if that holder is eligible and registers a Circle Mint account. — [Circle USDC Terms](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-terms)
- Access denial policy: Circle says it can block individual addresses from sending and receiving Circle stablecoin on every blockchain where that stablecoin is issued. A denied address can no longer send or receive, and all Circle stablecoin controlled by that address is blocked and cannot be transferred on-chain. Circle says it is not possible to deny access to individual tokens. The policy says Circle will not deny access except for: a threat to the security, integrity, or reliability of the stablecoin network, with a compromised minter key given as the example; a law, regulation, or legal order from a recognized U.S. or French authority, a U.S. or French court, or another government authority with jurisdiction over Circle; and certain urgent law-enforcement requests pending a court order, certain sanctions or imminent national-security requests, and certain orders pending a mutual legal assistance process. Reversal of a denial happens when that authority confirms the obligation is lifted, or when a security incident no longer requires it. A footnote says U.S. courts of competent jurisdiction are in Delaware or Massachusetts. — [Circle Stablecoin Access Denial Policy (PDF)](https://6778953.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/6778953/Circle%20Stablecoin%20Access%20Denial%20Policy_pdf.pdf)
- November 2026 operating description of a freeze: Circle says that when law enforcement obtains a valid court order to freeze a USDC wallet address, Circle freezes that address so USDC cannot move in or out, and Circle does not decide ownership, forfeiture, or whether a crime occurred. — [Circle, 5 November 2026](https://www.circle.com/blog/the-binance-incident-collective-defense-must-move-at-machine-speed)
- No deposit insurance, and no promise that 1 USDC trades at $1: the 15 October 2026 terms say Mint-account USDC is not FDIC- or SIPC-insured and not covered by the UK FSCS or an equivalent foreign scheme. Circle says it tokenizes and redeems at one dollar per USDC, less fees where applicable, but does not guarantee that one USDC equals one dollar on third-party platforms such as exchanges. Circle says it is not responsible for losses from those fluctuations, or for losses from using USDC on non-Circle platforms. — [Circle USDC Terms](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-terms)
- Solana mint: Circle’s contract-address page lists native USDC on Solana mainnet as `EPjFWdd5AufqSSqeM2qN1xzybapC8G4wEGGkZwyTDt1v`. The page warns that mainnet tokens have financial value and that integrators should verify addresses. — [Circle, USDC contract addresses](https://developers.circle.com/stablecoins/usdc-contract-addresses)
- Solana finality after 21 October 2026: Solana’s Alpenglow page says Alpenglow is live on mainnet and activated in epoch 1064, which started Wednesday 21 October 2026 at approximately 13:36 UTC. Under the previous Tower BFT consensus, finality took about 12.8 seconds. Alpenglow targets roughly 150 milliseconds, and the page says that figure is an expectation, not a constant. Under Votor, a block is final once a finalization certificate exists, and `confirmed` and `finalized` describe the same state. `processed` is unchanged and means only that the transaction executed in a bank. The page says execution, the SVM, programs, and fees are unchanged. — [Solana, Alpenglow](https://solana.com/upgrades/alpenglow)
- Anza’s commitment table for Alpenglow says Confirmed and Finalized are equivalent: both mean the transaction “has been permanently committed to the ledger and cannot be rolled back.” The table’s threshold is “60%+ stake voted finalize or 80%+ stake voted notarize.” The same page’s Tower BFT table, for the old protocol, still shows Confirmed as 66%+ stake voted, and Finalized as 31 or more confirmed blocks built on top. — [Anza, Solana Commitment Status](https://docs.anza.xyz/consensus/commitments)
- Conflicting older RPC wording still on solana.com: the `getTransaction` reference says `confirmed` is more stable than `processed` but “still a weaker guarantee than `finalized`,” and describes `finalized` as maximum vote lockout in vote towers. That is Tower BFT language. The Solana Platform payments doc still says to act on `finalized` for anything irreversible, and that a `confirmed` transaction “can, rarely, still be dropped.” Those two pages were not dated in the fetch. — [Solana RPC, getTransaction](https://solana.com/docs/rpc/http/gettransaction); [Solana Platform, Verifying a payment](https://docs.platform.solana.com/docs/payments/accept-verification)
- MiCA white paper, technology risk, as published by Circle: “Settlement Finality or Irrevocability of Blockchain Transactions. Depending on the tools and services providers used to initiate USDC transactions, these transactions may be irreversible.” The same white paper limits “USDC Holders” redemption rights against Circle SAS to holders located in the EEA, and tells holders outside the EEA to use the Circle LLC terms. It also describes a stress case in which Circle SAS may temporarily limit the maximum USDC redeemed in a period. That redemption limit is not a recall of a Solana transfer. — [Circle, MiCA USDC White Paper](https://www.circle.com/legal/mica-usdc-whitepaper)
- Fee sponsorship does not give the sponsor the token: Solana’s fee-abstraction doc says every transaction has a fee payer who pays the network fee in SOL. A sponsor can be that payer. Both must sign: the sender signs to authorize the token transfer, and the sponsor signs to pay the fee. The transfer instruction’s authority is the sender. The transaction-structure doc says the first signature is the fee payer and is also the transaction id. The fee payer must be a system-owned or nonce account and must hold enough lamports or the transaction fails with `InsufficientFundsForFee`. Kora’s operator doc says a compromised signer key can drain the sponsor’s SOL and sign unauthorized transactions. — [Solana, Fee Abstraction](https://solana.com/docs/payments/send-payments/payment-processing/fee-abstraction); [Solana, Transaction Structure](https://solana.com/docs/core/transactions/transaction-structure); [Solana, Kora signers](https://solana.com/docs/tools/kora/operators/signers)

### Inferences
- Waiting for `finalized` is the conservative check. Under Anza’s Alpenglow table, `confirmed` is the same commitment. The still-published RPC and payments pages that call `confirmed` weaker look unrevised. `processed` is not final on either description.
- A finalized transfer to a family member’s token account cannot be pulled back by the sender, by a fee sponsor, or by Circle recalling the transaction. Circle can later block that address so the USDC sitting there cannot be transferred. That strands the balance. It does not restore it to the sender. The access-denial policy says the balance “cannot be transferred on-chain,” not that Circle moves it to another wallet.
- The terms’ “freeze” sentence is narrower than the blocklist sentence: freeze of the balance is explicitly tied to Circle-custodied addresses, while on-chain blocking is a separate right. A self-custodied Solana wallet is not, on the face of that sentence, a Circle-custodied address. The on-chain block still matters, because the access-denial policy says it applies on every chain where the stablecoin is issued, and Solana is one of those chains on Circle’s address list.
- A fee sponsor who pays SOL cannot, by that role alone, move the user’s USDC. The sponsor becomes a loss mode only if the user signs the sponsor’s transaction, or if the sponsor’s key is also an authority on the token account. Kora’s warning about a compromised signer is about the sponsor’s SOL and any transaction that signer is willing to co-sign.

### Gaps
- The fetched Circle pages do not show the Solana mint’s freeze-authority address or a Solana-specific blacklist instruction. Third-party scanners describe Solana USDC as an SPL freeze of the holder’s token account, and they say that freeze stops outgoing transfers and is not a seizure. Those scanners are not Circle, and this research did not read the mint account. Do not treat “Circle can seize Solana USDC out of a wallet” as a sourced fact. PYUSD’s permanent delegate, mentioned on those same third-party pages, is a different token.
- A May 2025 Decrypt report that Circle froze about $58 million of USDC in Solana wallets tied to the Libra litigation was not used. Decrypt wrote that Circle did not comment. It is not a Circle confirmation.
- The access-denial PDF has no visible date, so it is not possible to say from the fetch whether it is newer or older than the 15 October 2026 terms.
- No Circle page fetched here says a Solana USDC transfer becomes reversible at `confirmed` or at `finalized`.

## What do Bridge and Crossmint document about MXN/SPEI payout failure, custody, and who can reverse a payout?

### Takeaway
Bridge documents SPEI payouts to any recipient, including a third party, as long as the customer has a SPEI endorsement and the destination is a Mexican CLABE. EU customers are excluded from that rail even though the MXN guide says customers in more than 100 countries are supported. Crossmint’s offramp pays only the verified user’s own bank, never a third party, and it documents an automatic USDC refund to the payer wallet if the bank rejects the payout. Neither provider documents a button that claws a successful SPEI credit back from the recipient.

### Cited Findings
- Bridge coverage versus customer eligibility: the MXN guide says Bridge supports virtual accounts for MXN deposits, transfers that on-ramp from a first party or a third-party business and off-ramp “to any recipient,” and liquidation addresses from stablecoin or crypto to MXN via SPEI. It says “We support MXN on/offramps for customers in +100 countries.” It also says a SPEI endorsement is required. The rail-specific page, for both SPEI on-ramps and off-ramps, says “Supported CLABE’s issued in Mexico” and “EU customers are not currently supported.” — [Bridge, MXN integration guide](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/get-started/guides/move-money/mxn-spei-integration-guide); [Bridge, Rail specific details](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/platform/orchestration/more/rail-specific)
- Bridge limits, from the rail page: SPEI on-ramp volume limits are 1,000,000 MXN for first-party deposits, 15,000 MXN for third-party deposits from individuals, and unlimited for third-party deposits from businesses. SPEI off-ramp volume limits are listed as unlimited for first-party withdrawals and for third-party withdrawals to individuals and to businesses. Transaction minimum is 50 MXN and there is no maximum, on both sides. The MXN guide’s logistics line also says no maximum, first- and third-party pay-ins and payouts, and that SPEI operates 24/7 and settles in seconds. The rail page adds that Bridge’s SPEI banking provider periodically does maintenance on Thursdays at 6:00 AM UTC for up to two hours, and that transactions may be paused then. — [Bridge, Rail specific details](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/platform/orchestration/more/rail-specific); [Bridge, MXN integration guide](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/get-started/guides/move-money/mxn-spei-integration-guide)
- Bridge account check: “Upon configuring an External Account, we will run a CLABE validation check. If the validation indicates that the CLABE account does not exist, we will automatically deactivate the External Account.” Creating a CLABE external account requires the SPEI endorsement; without it, creation is rejected. The guide’s off-ramp example uses `payment_rail: "spei"`, an `external_account_id`, and a `spei_reference` of at most 40 characters, alphanumeric and spaces. The sample off-ramp response is already `payment_submitted` and includes a `tracking_number`. The sample on-ramp deposit instructions name the CLABE account holder as “Bridge Building S.A. de C.V.” and the bank as “STP.” — [Bridge, MXN integration guide](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/get-started/guides/move-money/mxn-spei-integration-guide); [Bridge, External account validations](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/platform/orchestration/external-accounts/validations)
- Bridge reversal: cancellation “can only happen from the `awaiting_funds` state via the DELETE `/v0/transfers/{transferID}` endpoint.” `payment_processed` means funds were delivered. The happy path never goes backwards. `returned` means the payment was sent but failed, funds have returned to Bridge, and a refund to the sender is underway. `refunded` means Bridge no longer has the funds: they went back to the original sender, to the configured crypto return policy, or to the originating Bridge wallet or prefunded account. `missing_return_policy` means Bridge needs a crypto return policy before it can return a crypto deposit. For fiat, `refund_failed` means the original sending bank account is unreachable, and Bridge can only refund fiat deposits to that original account. — [Bridge, Transfer states](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/platform/orchestration/transfers/transfer-states)
- Crossmint who gets paid: “The destination bank account must belong to the same user who owns the wallet and completed verification.” “Offramp pays users out to their own bank accounts, never to third parties.” Bank details are tokenized at `vault.crossmint.com`. Crossmint keeps the token and the last digits, not the account number. The user broadcasts a prepared deposit transaction that carries a memo; Crossmint matches the deposit to the order by that memo. — [Crossmint, How It Works](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/concepts/how-it-works)
- Crossmint MXN as a destination, separate from a residence rule: the countries table lists Mexico (MX) with payout currency MXN and network SPEI. The Mexico detail row says SPEI expected arrival is instant, the window is 24/7, and there is no rail limit. Arrival estimates exclude the USDC deposit and conversion. Crossmint says its own limits may apply in addition to the rail limits shown. The same page’s only explicit residence gate in the fetched text is for SEPA: “Availability describes the receiving bank account, not the payer: at order time, Crossmint can refuse SEPA and SEPA Instant for a payer whose verified country of residence is outside the SEPA area.” The how-it-works page repeats a B2C SEPA residence check for the embedded component. The Mexico section does not state a residence requirement. — [Crossmint, Countries and Rails](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/supported-countries-and-rails); [Crossmint, How It Works](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/concepts/how-it-works)
- Crossmint product page versus the docs table: the product page says “80+ countries” and lists SPEI among rails. The docs page enumerates a shorter available-country table, which includes Mexico, the United States, and many SEPA countries, plus an “Upcoming Support” list that includes the United Kingdom, Guatemala, El Salvador, and others not in the available table. The docs page is the one that names Mexico/SPEI as available. — [Crossmint product page](https://www.crossmint.com/products/offramps); [Crossmint, Countries and Rails](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/supported-countries-and-rails)
- Crossmint failure and refund: Manage Orders says that if the bank rejects the payout after the stablecoin deposit has been received, the order is `completed` with a failed delivery and “the buyer deposit is automatically refunded.” The sample has `payment.refunded.amount` and `currency: "usdc"`, and `failureReason.code: "invalid-account"`. Other delivery failure codes listed are `transfer-failed`, `insufficient-funds`, `payout-unauthorized`, `slippage-tolerance-exceeded`, and `payment-refunded`. Quote expiry alone returns the order to the `quote` phase; if a deposit is seen before expiry but confirms after it, Crossmint refunds to the payer wallet rather than paying the stale rate. — [Crossmint, Manage Orders](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/guides/manage-orders)
- Crossmint says a success signal is not the last word on a bank return: create-order schema text says `completedAt` is “preserved if a completed payout later fails (e.g. a bank return)” and is absent if the payout never completed. On RTP, SEPA Instant, and SPEI, that timestamp is “the credit confirmation time.” — [Crossmint, Create Order](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/api-reference/create-order)
- Staging is not production: Crossmint says staging payouts are simulated and succeed unless a special test account is used. The test accounts that always fail are a US account (routing `021000021`, account `123456789012`) and a SEPA IBAN. The staging failure example ends `phase: "completed"` with `delivery.status: "failed"` and `payment.status: "completed"`, and that particular JSON sample does not include `payment.refunded`. The docs say to treat that staging shape like a production bank rejection and to read `delivery.status`, and they point to Manage Orders for the refund behavior. They also say those test numbers are ordinary accounts in production and must not be shipped. — [Crossmint, Test a Failed Payout](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/guides/test-payout-failures); [Crossmint, How It Works](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/concepts/how-it-works)
- A saved Crossmint MX account is usable only when active: the collect-bank-accounts example for `bank-account-mx-clabe` shows `rails.supported: ["spei"]`. The guide says `rails.pending` cannot be used yet, a method can list rails while still `pending`, only an `active` method accepts an order, and a valid account with no rail is `rejected` with `statusReason: "no-rail-available"`. — [Crossmint, Manage Payment Methods](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/guides/collect-bank-accounts)

### Inferences
- Residency and payout coverage are different checks on both providers. On Bridge, “customers in +100 countries” is customer eligibility, “EU customers are not currently supported” is a customer exclusion on the SPEI rail, and “CLABE issued in Mexico” is destination coverage. A non-EU customer can be eligible while a European customer is not, and in either case the bank account that receives MXN still has to be a Mexican CLABE. On Crossmint, the countries table is about the receiving account. The only residence rule written on the fetched pages is the SEPA payer-residence refusal. Absence of a Mexico residence sentence is not evidence that Crossmint ignores residence in KYC; it is only evidence that this page does not state one.
- A family payout to a relative’s Mexican bank matches the flow Bridge documents (“offramp to any recipient,” third-party off-ramps to individuals listed as unlimited) and does not match the flow Crossmint documents (“never to third parties”). Using Crossmint for that relative would require the relative to be the verified user, paying out from their own wallet to their own CLABE.
- Neither API documents the sender reversing a SPEI credit that has already succeeded. Bridge’s undo is cancel-before-funding, or a provider refund if the payment fails or returns. Crossmint’s undo of a failed payout is a refund of USDC to the payer wallet, and its schema allows a later bank return after `completedAt`. A bank return is the rail or the receiving bank sending the money back, not the family app recalling it.
- The name on Bridge’s sample SPEI deposit account is Bridge’s Mexican entity, not the end user. That is as far as the docs go on who holds the pesos before conversion. They do not state deposit insurance, and this note does not add any.

### Gaps
- No Bridge legal agreement, SPEI scheme rule, or Banxico document was fetched on whether a beneficiary bank can reverse a settled SPEI credit, on what “settles in seconds” means in scheme terms, or on how long a `returned` state can appear after `payment_processed`. The state machine says the happy path never goes backwards. It does not say a later banking return is impossible.
- No Crossmint page fetched here says whether a refunded USDC amount can be less than the deposit after fees, or which chain and address receive the refund, beyond “the payer wallet.”
- The staging failed-payout sample omits `payment.refunded`, while Manage Orders says a production bank rejection is automatically refunded. The docs tell the reader to follow Manage Orders. They do not explain the missing field in the staging sample.
- Bridge’s “+100 countries” list was not enumerated in the fetched guide, so it is not possible to say from these pages whether the United States or Mexico is inside that set except by the EU exclusion and the Mexican-CLABE destination rule.
- Custody of USDC between a Bridge liquidation address and the SPEI payout, and the legal entity that holds customer USDC, was not in the pages fetched. The virtual-account example shows the MXN deposit account holder, not the crypto custodian.

## Which controls are enforced by a wallet, a program, or a provider, and which are only user-interface warnings?

### Takeaway
A control is real only if a missing signature, a program rule, or the provider’s API rejects the action. On Solana, the token authority’s signature and finalized commitment are the boundary. Circle’s address block is an issuer control on the token, not a wallet warning. Bridge and Crossmint enforce endorsement, first-party rules, and account checks in their APIs. A confirmation dialog does not stop a signed transaction or a submitted payout.

### Cited Findings
- Solana runtime, fee payer versus token authority: the fee payer is the first signer and pays SOL fees. A different signer, the token authority, authorizes the transfer. Both signatures are required when a sponsor pays the fee. The runtime rejects a fee payer that is not a system or nonce account, or that lacks lamports, with `InsufficientFundsForFee`. — [Solana, Transaction Structure](https://solana.com/docs/core/transactions/transaction-structure); [Solana, Fee Abstraction](https://solana.com/docs/payments/send-payments/payment-processing/fee-abstraction); [Solana, Account Runtime](https://solana.com/docs/core/accounts/account-runtime)
- Solana finality as a boundary: under Alpenglow, Anza says a confirmed or finalized transaction is permanently committed and cannot be rolled back. `processed` only means the transaction executed in a bank. Solana’s upgrade page dates that consensus change to 21 October 2026. — [Anza, Commitment Status](https://docs.anza.xyz/consensus/commitments); [Solana, Alpenglow](https://solana.com/upgrades/alpenglow)
- Issuer boundary, not a wallet warning: Circle’s access-denial policy says a denied address cannot send or receive Circle stablecoin and the stablecoin it controls cannot be transferred on-chain, on every chain where it is issued. The 15 October 2026 terms separate that on-chain block from Circle’s inability to recall a transaction once initiated. The 5 November 2026 blog says a court-ordered freeze stops USDC moving in or out of the address. — [Circle Access Denial Policy](https://6778953.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/6778953/Circle%20Stablecoin%20Access%20Denial%20Policy_pdf.pdf); [Circle USDC Terms](https://www.circle.com/legal/usdc-terms); [Circle, 5 November 2026](https://www.circle.com/blog/the-binance-incident-collective-defense-must-move-at-machine-speed)
- What a signer sees can be a false boundary: on 21 February 2025 Bybit signers approved a Safe transaction whose UI was disguised. Safe’s 28 February 2025 statement says the review did not find a smart-contract vulnerability; the failure was a compromised developer machine proposing a disguised transaction. Safe’s remaining instruction to users is to exercise extreme caution when signing. — [Bybit timeline](https://www.bybit.com/en/learn/this-week-in-bybit/bybit-security-incident-timeline); [Safe statement](https://safefoundation.org/blog/safe-ecosystem-foundation-statement)
- Bridge API boundaries: SPEI endorsement is required or the external account is rejected. A CLABE that fails existence validation is deactivated. A transfer can be canceled only in `awaiting_funds`. After `payment_processed`, the documented state machine does not move backward. Fiat refunds go only to the original sending account. The Thursday 06:00 UTC maintenance window can pause transactions. EU customers are rejected for this rail regardless of a Mexican CLABE. — [Bridge validations](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/platform/orchestration/external-accounts/validations); [Bridge MXN guide](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/get-started/guides/move-money/mxn-spei-integration-guide); [Bridge transfer states](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/platform/orchestration/transfers/transfer-states); [Bridge rail details](https://apidocs.bridge.xyz/platform/orchestration/more/rail-specific)
- Crossmint API boundaries: the order resolves KYC through the verified user who owns the wallet, and the destination account must be that user’s. A payment method accepts an order only when `active`. A rail still `pending` cannot be used. A memo on the deposit binds it to the order. Quote expiry causes a refund rather than a payout at the old rate if the deposit confirms late. A bank rejection is a provider refund of the deposit, not an on-chain recall by the user. The embedded component refuses removal of a saved bank account once that session’s withdrawal was sent, and while another withdrawal from the same wallet is still confirming. That refusal is a component rule, described in the docs as component behavior. — [Crossmint, How It Works](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/concepts/how-it-works); [Crossmint, Manage Payment Methods](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/guides/collect-bank-accounts); [Crossmint, Manage Orders](https://docs.crossmint.com/offramp/guides/manage-orders)

### Inferences
- For a family Solana transfer, the enforced stack is: the owner’s signature on the token instruction, the sponsor’s signature only for the fee, and cluster commitment at `finalized`. An address-book name, a “send to Mom?” modal, or a parsed-simulation banner is a warning unless the app refuses to build the transaction without a separate check that is actually on the wire. The Bybit incident is the case where the modal and the signed bytes were different, and the chain enforced the bytes.
- Circle’s block is enforced when the USDC program or contract rejects the transfer. It is not enforced by the family app’s UI. It also does not create an undo for a transfer that already finalized to an address Circle has not blocked.
- Bridge’s CLABE existence check is a provider control at account setup. It is not a check that the account belongs to the relative whose name the app displayed. The docs say the check asks whether the CLABE exists, then deactivates the external account if it does not. They do not describe a name-match guarantee in the pages fetched.
- Crossmint’s first-party rule is a provider control. It stops the demo from paying a relative’s bank unless that relative is the KYC’d wallet owner. It does not stop that user from sending USDC on Solana to someone else’s wallet before the offramp. That send is outside Crossmint and is bound by Solana signatures and finality.
- Fee sponsorship is a real signature requirement for the fee, and only for the fee. Treating the sponsor as a custodian, or as someone who can reverse the user’s transfer, is not what the Solana docs say.

### Gaps
- No source fetched here inventories which consumer Solana wallets show a warning versus which refuse to sign. Wallet warning copy was not reviewed, so no specific wallet modal is labeled as UI-only by its vendor.
- Circle’s Solana freeze-authority address was not read from the mint account at `EPjFWdd5AufqSSqeM2qN1xzybapC8G4wEGGkZwyTDt1v`, so the precise on-chain instruction that implements “block the transfer” on Solana is not cited from Circle or from a mint read.
- Bridge and Crossmint docs do not label their dashboard confirmation screens as enforceable or decorative. The enforceable statements above are the API rules: endorsement, account state, cancel window, first-party recipient, active payment method, and refund-on-failure.
